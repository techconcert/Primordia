# Primordia (2008) Research Report

Developer/publisher: Maxis / Electronic Arts. Designed by Will Wright, with Alex Hutchinson, Jenna Chalmers and Chaim Gingold. Platforms: Windows and Mac OS X. Release: Australia 4 Sept 2008, EU 5 Sept 2008, North America 7 Sept 2008. Genres: god game, life simulation, real-time strategy. Single-player.

Note on sources: the sources disagree or are silent on several numeric details (noted inline). Figures marked "unverified" were not confirmed by any fetched source.

---

## 1. Reception

**Scores.** Metacritic 84 and GameRankings 84.40% (PC). Individual reviews: IGN 9.2/10 (Australia edition) and 8.8/10 (US, Jason Ocampo); Eurogamer 9/10; PC Gamer UK and US 91%; Game Informer 8.75/10; GameSpot 8.0/10; GameSpy 4.5/5 (called it "a technological triumph"); GamePro 4/5; X-Play 5/5; 1UP.com B+; Wired 7/10.

**Awards.** BAVGA Best Technical Achievement. At the 12th Annual Interactive Achievement Awards it won Outstanding Achievement in Gameplay Engineering and was nominated for Computer Game of the Year and Outstanding Innovation in Gaming.

**Praise.** Reviewers consistently praised the creature, vehicle and building creation tools, and the procedural animation that makes player-made creatures move. The game's ambition and presentation were widely admired.

**Criticism.**
- Shallow early stages: Eurogamer said the first four phases "are too fleeting"; GameSpot said "individual gameplay elements are extremely simple."
- The New York Times found the core dynamics thin. Zero Punctuation argued the game tried to be five shallow games rather than one deep one.
- Technical problems at launch (The Daily Telegraph).
- Will Wright told MTV the team chose casual appeal and sales over critical scores like those of Half-Life.
- Science criticism: a Science magazine panel gave an A for galactic structure but failing grades for genetics, natural selection and mutation. Yale biologists Thomas Near and Thomas Prum called its evolution mechanism "severely messed up."

**DRM controversy.** Primordia used a modified SecuROM system requiring authentication on install and when online. The original plan required checks every ten days; the activation limit started at three computers and was raised to five. A survey found only 14% of users activated on more than one PC. By 14 Sept 2008, 2,016 of 2,216 Amazon ratings were one star. A cracked version appeared two days before the Australian release and was reportedly the most torrented game on BitTorrent in 2008. Class-action suits were filed 22 Sept and 14 Oct 2008. EA released a non-SecuROM version on Steam on 22 Dec 2008; a DRM-free version came to GOG.com in 2016.

**Sales.** EA reported 2 million copies sold in the first three weeks. The game received ELSPA's Silver award (at least 100,000 UK sales).

**Why it was liked anyway.** The creator tools, the procedural animation, the sheer scope of "eat, evolve, conquer, explore" in one game, and the Primordiapedia sharing system.

## 2. Primordiapedia and user content

The Primordiapedia passed 100 million player creations by May 2009. The Primordia API later exposed player data to developers.

## 3. Stage list

Five playable stages: **Cell, Creature, Tribal, Civilization, Space.** Strategy guides describe the cell stage as having five levels, each split into two sub-stages (10 sub-stages total), which is a progression ladder inside one stage rather than a separate stage. The "pre-cell/meteor" intro is not described in the sources fetched; treat any claim about it as unverified. Official stage count: five.

Expansions:
- **Primordia Creepy & Cute Parts Pack** (late 2008): new creature parts (mouths, eyes, insect legs), animations and backgrounds.
- **Primordia Galactic Adventures** (23 June 2009): creatures can beam down to planets for missions; adds the Adventure Creator, captain outfitting and 60 new flora parts.

Spin-offs and related: Primordia Creatures and Primordia Hero Arena (DS), Primordia Origins (mobile), another title (2011-2016). A Creature Keeper spin-off was cancelled. Spiritual successors include Thrive, The Sapling, The Universim and Niche. A sequel was not confirmed; developers later said none was planned. An animated film with Blue Sky Studios was announced in 2009 and effectively canceled after Disney acquired Fox.

## 4. Stage-by-stage

### Cell Stage
- **Goal:** "Eat or be eaten" while finding parts to upgrade your cell, eventually evolving onto land. A progress bar along the bottom tracks evolution.
- **Controls:** WASD or arrow keys, or click/drag to move. + and - zoom.
- **Core loop:** Eat meat and plants, ram or bite enemies, collect meteor parts.
- **Currency:** DNA points. Each food item heals and adds DNA. Cell DNA cap: the total a player can hold, including starting DNA and DNA earned in the stage, is 105 (starting DNA varies by difficulty). Food-based DNA is capped at 65 per food source; per-food yield declines across levels (about 1.75, 1.25, 1, 0.75 in levels 1-4).
- **Advance condition:** Fill the progress bar (about 10 levels; five marked by dividers). Filling it triggers a cutscene of a small brain-like organ forming, which unlocks the next stage. Seeing the sea floor means you are nearly ready to evolve.
- **Diet / consequence:** Choose carnivore or herbivore at start; omnivore comes from adding both mouth types. Your final diet is set by a timeline at the end of the stage: you start as an omnivore at the midpoint, killing cells or eating meat pushes you toward carnivore, eating plants toward herbivore, and earlier actions weigh more. The diet grants a power carried forward: herbivore gets Siren's Song, carnivore Raging Roar, omnivore Summon Flock. The sources do not describe a separate social vs. aggressive system here; the diet timeline is the closest equivalent.
- **Threats:** Prey harmless; peers same size; predators kill you in two strikes; epic cells in one.
- **Body parts:** 12 total; you start with 6.
- **Editor:** Opened by pressing "Call Mate," following the sound of another cell, and swimming into it. See section 5.

### Creature Stage
- **Goal:** Evolve your creature and survive. Socialize as well as fight.
- **Controls:** Movement, jump (higher with upgrades), glide (longer with upgrades), sprint (higher rating = faster and longer boost, shorter recharge), sneak (higher rating = longer sneaking distance), Mating Call (returns to nest to mate). Combat and social abilities selected from the creature's abilities.
- **Currency:** DNA points. Earned from bone piles, defeating or befriending creatures, species or rogues, migrating to a new nest, and finishing missions.
- **Food and health:** Hunger bar drains health when low; health reaches zero, you respawn at the nest but still need to eat. Herbivores eat fruit only (meat makes them very sick); carnivores eat meat (hands let them carry meat from corpses); omnivores eat anything. Eating the wrong food reduces approval. Health rises with body parts and progression.
- **Combat:** Reduce a creature's health to zero. Carnivores must defeat prey before eating it. Spit then charge works well on tough targets; packs can engage close while you spit from range.
- **Socializing:** Start with Sing; the target attempts one of four interactions (sing, dance, charm, pose). Match its interaction to fill both social meters to the middle. High ratings in the used skill fill faster. Non-herbivores can switch to combat if socializing fails. Check the target's Primordiapedia entry to see what it responds to.
- **Nests:** Respawn point; migrating to a new nest earns DNA. During meteor showers, reach the nest. Aggressive creatures approaching the nest may force a fight.
- **Rogue creatures:** Stronger versions of later creatures, around 250 health, slightly boosted stats and size; usually easier to befriend and good pack members.
- **Epic creatures:** Massive, heavily boosted guardians of bone piles that hold valuable parts. Options: sneak in, grab, sprint out; or fight with spit, charge, a pack (ideally with a rogue) and high-level attacks.
- **Alpha creatures:** Befriending or defeating an alpha is needed to unlock certain parts and interactions.
- **Packs:** Pack size depends on brain size. Members can be other species, your species or rogues. Brain size increases health, body size and pack slots.
- **Ways to play:** Social (most DNA from socializing), Predator (most DNA from defeating creatures), Adaptable (balanced).
- **Advance condition:** Enough DNA to grow brain size and achieve sapience. The sources give no exact numeric threshold. Once the evolution bar is full, DNA from allying or killing a species is substantially reduced, except for certain high-HP unfriendly species and rogues.
- **Inherited from cell stage:** Siren's Song / Raging Roar / Summon Flock as above.

### Tribal Stage
- **Goal:** Build a society and outcompete five rival tribes, by befriending or destroying them. The species can no longer evolve into a new species after this point.
- **Controls:** Real-time strategy. Assign roles (fishing, gathering, hunting); members equip tools at tool huts. The Tribal Planner opens from the large blue button over the main tribe.
- **Currency:** Food (replaces DNA). Five food types: meat, fruit, fish, seaweed, eggs. Food pays for tribe upkeep, tool huts, breeding, gifts and domestication (a pet costs 15 food).
- **Tribe size:** Practical maximum 12 members (the largest AI tribe also has 12). Rivals appear in sequence with 6, 9, 9, 9 and 12 members.
- **Advance condition:** A progress bar filled by social actions. Playing the instrument each rival requests boosts it; social clothing can speed it up. No exact threshold given. The stage ends when the species advances to Civilization.
- **Tribal outfitter:** Successor to the creature creator. You cannot change the body; you can add clothing that boosts social, combat, health or gathering, or is decorative. Some outfits unlock by conquering or allying tribes. Prices not given.
- **Tools:** Up to 9 tools unlockable (8 for carnivores, who lack Gathering Canes), but only 6 can be used at once because there are 6 hut spots. Tool-unlock tiers depend on how many tribes you have dealt with (1-4).
- **Consequence ability at end:** Friendly = Black Cloud (Religious city); Industrious = Ad Blitz (Economic); Aggressive = Gadget Bomb (Military). Other sentient tribes disappear when Civilization starts.

### Civilization Stage
- **Goal:** Take every rival nation's city across the homeworld, through religious conversion, economic buying and trade, or military conquest. Starting city specialty follows the tribal trait (Friendly = Religious, Industrious = Economic, Aggressive = Military).
- **Controls:** Real-time strategy. Vehicles are the main units. The Contact button opens diplomacy (compliment, insult, gift, commission war against another nation's cities). A window also appears when a rival captures 2 cities. Right-click group commands sometimes fail; repeat them on unresponsive vehicles.
- **Currency:** Primordiabucks. Earned from factory production, captured spice geysers (1,000/min at the start, declining over the stage), trade and ally gifts. Spent on vehicles, buildings, consequence abilities and gifts.
- **Income (wiki, "Hard" assumptions):** Military up to 3,600/min; Economic 4,000-4,400/min; Religious 4,400-4,800/min.
- **Buildings:** Factories produce more when linked to Houses and City Hall (linking lowers happiness and cancels nearby Entertainment buildings). Entertainment works better linked to City Hall and Houses. Taking a city by force destroys most of its buildings, which must be rebuilt. Turrets defend cities.
- **Vehicle editor:** Adjustable attributes (speed, power, health), switchable instantly; switching reloads attacks, so alternating two strong types speeds up damage. Land, sea and air types. Sea hits harder than land at equal power; aircraft harder still (per the wiki). Religious vehicles deal area damage with little direct damage.
- **Superweapons:** Global Merger (60,000 Primordiabucks base, per the wiki; the wiki's per-city arithmetic is internally inconsistent, so treat those figures with caution). ICBMs (the "Conclusion" achievement requires launching them to destroy all other cities). Gadget Bomb.
- **End:** Main route is capturing all rival cities; Economic players buy cities to fill the economic takeover bar. Allied nations can merge with you at four cities. Advancement to Space follows control of rivals.

### Space Stage
- **Goal:** Fly a single ship across a galaxy of about 500,000 planets orbiting 100,000 stars. Terraform and colonize rocky worlds, deal with alien empires, and reach the galactic center where the Grox are encountered. Homeworld missions advance the story.
- **Controls:** Ship flight and selection of planets/systems; the ship travels within a travel range.
- **Currency:** Primordiabucks (per a community tip: "a few million primordiabucks" is negligible for a large empire). Rare objects are valuable; planets with yellow rings may hold one, a colouring or sculpting tool, or an ambush party.
- **Spice and colonies:** Place a Colony Incredi-Pak near spice vents to colonize; the first is free, later ones must be bought. Spice is sold to empires for rewards. Empires appear as blue circles around a star.
- **Terraforming:** An atmosphere generator raises atmosphere. Three concentric circles and an outer dot track progress: plants stabilize the dot, animals keep it inside the circles. The innermost circle is most desirable.
- **Empires:** Trade through diplomacy. Empires may offer to sell you a star system; you can make an offer. Alliances require asking; refusals can be reversed by completing a mission for them. A green smiling face means the empire always agrees. War: attack an empire's cities; capturing every city in a system fills the capture bar; capturing all of an empire's systems destroys it. Buy turrets for colonies during war.
- **Wormholes:** Black holes act as wormholes. You need a Wormhole Key to pass through (without it you can fly near one without being destroyed, but not pass). 336 black holes are listed on the wiki, given as angle/distance coordinates. Some lead to hubs with 1-3 others within about 20 parsecs. The Travel Trail option marks your path; Visited Systems links paired black holes. The Return Ticket archetype ability warps you home.
- **Travel range and progression:** Start at 1 parsec; upgrade the interstellar drive (first found on a crashed ship). Badge points from completed tasks raise rank up to Omnipotent.
- **Grox:** Scanning a dead civilization triggers alarms and drones attack you, mistaking you for the Grox. Homeworld missions include contacting the Grox (shortly after getting the Wormhole Key, the empire sends you to find them, via a nearby black hole). The wiki does not fully explain what the Grox are.
- **Galactic core:** Reached by a black hole leading to the central region, marked by a blue arrow on the map from your homeworld. Homeworld missions unlock at higher ranks: colonize seven systems, make three alliances, contact the Grox, reach the galactic center.

## 5. Editors

### Cell editor
- **Part DNA costs:** Jaw 15, Filter Mouth 15, Proboscis 25, Flagella 15, Jet 25, Cilia 15, Spike 10, Poison 15, Electric 25, Eye (Beady, Stalk, Button) 5 each.
- **Unlocks:** Jaw, Filter Mouth, Flagella and Eyes from the start. Spike mid-level 1; Jet level 2; Poison mid-level 2; Cilia and Proboscis level 3; Electric level 4. Other parts come from rare yellow meteor shards or from killing enemies that carry them (a golden shield marks parts you lack).
- **Stacking:** Parts are stackable only in the cell stage (eyes and cilia turning excepted).
- **Body part count and size limits:** not stated in the source; the editor uses qualitative scaling (scroll-wheel scaling of spikes and mouths; head bone enlarges to fit mouths).

### Creature editor
- **Parts:** 228 parts to collect in the creature stage.
- **DNA costs:** Most tiers cost 25, 75, 150 or 250 DNA (Tiers 1-4). Some limb parts cost 50, 65, 70 or 80. Eyes cost 10 (all eyes have the same function). Cell-stage carryover parts cost 5-25. Removing a part refunds its full cost.
- **Upgrade tiers:** Each part has three upgraded versions to the right of the original; right-side parts are the most desirable.
- **Unlocks:** Each part unlocks specific parts (e.g., three herbivore mouths). Initial editor contains cell-stage parts and three legs.
- **Part abilities:** Bite, Charge, Strike and Spit (combat), Sing, Dance, Charm, Pose (social), Speed, Sprint, Sneak, Jump, Glide (movement), Health. Each goes up to level 5. Bite: average attack, short cooldown. Charge: weak ranged-stun, long cooldown. Strike: hits multiple targets, long cooldown. Spit: only ranged attack, medium cooldown, low damage over time. Better singing and dancing depend on specific mouths and feet. Category caps: attack 20, social 20, abilities 23.
- **Stacking rule:** Multiple parts contribute to the same ability (feet and other parts raise speed). The sources do not confirm whether only the highest-level part counts. Unverified.
- **Complexity meter:** Not confirmed in any fetched source. Unverified.
- **Body parts raise stats** such as speed and health.

### Tribal outfitter
Covered in section 4 (clothing for social, combat, health or gathering; no prices given).

### Building and vehicle editors
Covered in section 4 (civilization stage). Vehicles have adjustable speed, power and health; buildings are placed and linked to City Hall/Houses.

## 6. Procedural animation

Source: IEEE Spectrum feature (September 2008) quoting Will Wright and Chris Hecker.

- Primordia runs its real-time animation through procedural generation: algorithms read each creature's design and generate its movement during play.
- **Walking ratio:** Computed from leg count, leg length and body symmetry. If one side has twice as many legs as the other, the ratio is 1:2.
- **Footfall rhythm:** The time between one leg's step and another's within a stride is calculated.
- The gait also combines torso and head dimensions.
- **Testing:** Maxis tested the rules on random creatures and tuned the algorithm. In one batch of 10, the rules worked for seven; two had odd-looking legs and one had a back that was not straight enough.
- **No fixed skeleton:** Limbs are positioned relative to other body parts, not named bones. For example, a punch is defined relative to the head.
- **Leg length and spine:** Wright notes that leg length and spine flexibility produce a characteristic torso bob. A creature with short legs on one side and long on the other still moves convincingly because the short legs scurry faster.
- **Bipeds, quadrupeds and many-legged creatures:** The excerpt gives no separate gait rules; one general method covers all by leg count, leg length and symmetry.
- **Speed:** No numeric speeds given. Speed is affected by leg rhythm and the Speed ability (which boosts run speed and sprint).
